Supporting AGLC5 Adoption

An open letter to the AGLC5 Committee: evidence from a complete AGLC4 implementation, an offer of collaboration, and a proposal for licensing the fifth edition for the digital age.

Revised August 2026 · read the original letter (April 2026)

To:

  • The Melbourne University Law Review Association
  • The Melbourne Journal of International Law
  • The AGLC5 Committee

Citation standards and citation tools have historically been developed independently. A guide is published; tool authors then reverse-engineer the rules into software; researchers wait, sometimes years, for their tools to catch up. With each edition the gap widens, because the rules grow more nuanced while the tools’ data models stay the same. The demand is real: two independent AGLC4 patches, both generated with the assistance of generative AI, are currently hosted on GitHub and in use by Australian legal researchers. That is not because existing tools are poorly made, but because the distance between what the AGLC requires and what any tool can produce without guidance from the standard’s authors is too great for the community to bridge alone.

Obiter is a free, open-source Microsoft Word add-in that implements the full AGLC4 specification across all 26 chapters: 77 source types, a 302-item chapter-by-chapter conformance audit, and around 3,300 unit tests, each pinned to a specific rule or worked example. Building it rule by rule is the strictest reading a text can receive: every ambiguity becomes a forced decision, and every decision is recorded. This letter shares what that process found, engages with the Committee’s announced themes, and makes one further proposal about how the fifth edition is published and licensed. I note the Committee’s website records that consultations ran across 2023 to 2026 and that it is now considering the outcomes; this letter is offered as correspondence in that spirit, in the hope the material below is useful while drafting decisions are still open.

What a Complete Implementation Revealed

Implementing a standard mechanically is a form of quality assurance no proofread can match, and it is offered here in that spirit. AGLC4 is a mature and widely adopted standard; its defects are not fatal. But they are numerous, and their shape is systematic. The consolidated catalogue, published in the Obiter repository in paraphrased form with pinpoint references out of respect for the Review’s copyright, records:

  • Roughly 78 anomalies in the worked examples, the guide’s weakest surface: examples that contradict their own rule or table, factual slips (wrong weekdays for stated dates, a misspelled translator, a 1949–1949 year span, duplicated proceeding numbers), and cross-references that point at the wrong rule. Around 30 are confirmably wrong against the corrected printings, and about 29 are self-contradictions in which the guide disagrees with itself.
  • Eight rules that cannot be applied mechanically as written. The most striking: rule 1.8.3 makes italicisation of foreign terms turn on membership of the latest Macquarie Dictionary, a proprietary, paywalled work with no edition named, so the test cannot be authoritatively performed by anyone; and rule 4.2 requires italics inside title strings that no plain citation data model can represent.
  • At least 19 under-specifications where a rule exists only as a single illustration or an undefined category (“non-numeric issue”, “named lecture”, “revised edition”), or is buried in a prose note, each forcing implementers into silent judgment calls that will differ from tool to tool.
  • Forty-five inconsistencies across the appendices, including authorised-report markers that conflict with the rule chapters, an undefined marker legend, and single abbreviations that each denote several distinct report series.
  • Small self-application gaps that illustrate the pattern: the guide bans spaced em-dashes in citations while using them in its own Part headings, and one journal-title example converts an ampersand that the governing rule says to reproduce.

None of this is unusual for a work of this complexity; the 17-page erratum published within nine months of AGLC4’s release shows the editors already correct at scale. The point is that mechanical testing during drafting, something tool implementers do as a matter of course, would catch most of this before publication rather than after. The full catalogue, together with every implementation decision it forced, is public and offered to the Committee as input.

The Opportunity

Since its first edition in 1998, the Australian Guide to Legal Citation has provided the consistency and rigour that Australian legal scholarship depends on. Earlier editions did not fail; they simply predated the tooling ecosystem that exists today. AGLC5 can be the first edition designed with implementability as a drafting consideration. Three areas illustrate what that collaboration could look like:

  • Generative AI citation. AGLC4 predates ChatGPT and contains no rule for AI output. The Review’s editors issued interim guidance citing AI output broadly following rule 7.12 (written correspondence), and every major Australian university law library has propagated that workaround. No citation tool can emit a correct GenAI citation today because no rule authorises one.
  • Subsequent references. The above-n cross-reference system is among the most common patterns researchers use and among the hardest to automate reliably, because the rules do not specify a computable criterion for when short titles should appear or how short titles for whole Acts differ from those for portions of Acts.
  • International materials. The international chapters (rules 8 to 14) have never been fully implemented in any citation tool, because standard metadata models have no natural fields for these material types. Comprehensiveness in the guide only reaches researchers if it can be represented in software.

Evidence from the Digital Age

Since AGLC4 was finalised in 2018, every peer standard with a recent edition has taken a position on generative AI, and Australian courts have built an entire regulatory layer around it. Both developments point to the same design.

Peer standards. OSCOLA’s fifth edition (published March 2026) adds a generative-AI rule, 3.7.13, citing the tool as author with the prompt, developer, and generation date, and reinstates a substantially revised public international law section. The Bluebook’s 22nd edition (May 2025) introduced rule 18.3 for AI content and promptly drew published criticism from scholars and practitioners for over-engineering: for imposing screenshot-preservation burdens, sitting awkwardly with confidentiality and work-product obligations, and treating a research tool as a citable authority. The emerging consensus of that criticism, “disclose, don’t cite”, matches the pattern in APA, MLA, Chicago, and the ICMJE: reuse an existing template with a source-type descriptor for the rare case where AI output is itself the object of discussion, and handle routine AI assistance through a separate disclosure track. Canada shows the cost of standing still: the McGill Guide’s tenth edition (2023) has no GenAI rule, and a community standard, the Canadian Open Access Legal Citation Guide, now supplies the format the incumbent lacks.

Australian courts. Between late 2024 and mid 2026, the courts constructed the disclosure-and-verification layer themselves: the Supreme Court of NSW’s Practice Note SC Gen 23 (commenced February 2025, with parallel notes across the NSW District Court, Local Court, NCAT and the Personal Injury Commission), Victoria’s Practice Note SC Gen 25 (May 2026, replacing the 2024 guidelines), Queensland’s accuracy directions requiring a named practitioner to verify every cited authority (September 2025, mirrored in the District Court, Planning and Environment Court, and QCAT), the Federal Court’s GPN-AI (April 2026), and equivalents in the FCFCOA, South Australia and Western Australia. The lesson for an academic citation guide is clean: courts now govern disclosure of AI assistance; AGLC5’s job is citation of AI output, and conflating the two, as the Bluebook did, is the documented failure mode to avoid.

Institutional currency. The guide’s institutional data has also aged. Since 2018: the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia commenced 1 September 2021 (Act No 12 of 2021); the Administrative Review Tribunal replaced the AAT on 14 October 2024 (Act No 40 of 2024); the National Anti-Corruption Commission commenced 1 July 2023 (Act No 88 of 2022); the NSW Personal Injury Commission commenced 1 March 2021; TASCAT consolidated nine Tasmanian tribunals on 5 November 2021; and the Queensland Reports abbreviation changed from Qd R to QR in March 2020, a change the current appendix records but the rule chapter does not. AGLC5 could resolve this class of problem permanently with a dated institutional-succession appendix, so that legacy citations remain unambiguous every time the machinery of government changes.

The Six AGLC5 Themes

Each of the Committee’s announced themes intersects with implementability: comprehensiveness only reaches researchers when tools can represent the new material types; structural clarity directly determines whether rules can be encoded as deterministic logic; simplification helps human users and software equally, with the subsequent-reference system the clearest candidate; First Nations citation involves controlled vocabularies whose data model must preserve names across languages and scripts with the care they deserve; the digital-age theme is the natural home for the GenAI rule, archived-URL and preservation metadata, and dataset and software types; and because sample citations are the test corpus tool authors build against, inclusive and accurate examples carry normative weight in software from day one.

Licensing the Fifth Edition

One further question deserves the Committee’s attention alongside the rules themselves: how AGLC5 is licensed. I raise it as the maintainer of a tool whose entire transcription discipline, paraphrase only, no verbatim rule text, no guide examples in the interface, exists solely because of the current licensing posture, and every forced paraphrase is a small divergence risk in a standard whose purpose is uniformity.

Three observations frame the choice. First, copyright has never protected the citation system itself; systems and methods sit outside copyright, so what the Review owns is the prose, the examples, and the arrangement. Second, the digital shift has already ended pay-to-read: the AGLC4 PDF has been free since 2018, with print surviving as a convenience good. What remains enclosed is reuse: quoting rule text in tools and teaching materials, and machine-readable encodings. That is precisely the layer a citation standard needs open to do its job, and Australia’s fair dealing regime, which has no general quotation exception, gives implementers no safe way through. Third, the precedents are instructive. Enclosure of the Bluebook produced the public-domain Indigo Book; the McGill Guide’s paywall produced the community COAL guide, which then out-modernised the incumbent on AI. AGLC has no fork, which is exactly the window in which to choose official-open over eventual unofficial-open.

A model that keeps openness and revenue aligned might look like this: the text of the guide released under a licence such as CC BY-ND, where the no-derivatives term protects canonical integrity, the Review’s legitimate concern; a low-cost commercial reuse licence so that large commercial integrators pay while open-source and educational reuse stays free; and the Review’s revenue anchored in authority rather than scarcity, through print as artefact, institutional licences for learning-platform and publisher embedding, a certification program for conformant tools, and, above all, an official machine-readable ruleset and conformance test suite licensed to commercial implementers. OSCOLA has long made its text freely available online alongside paid print, without evident harm to either. The Review would lose nothing it currently monetises and would gain the standard-setter’s true asset: every tool in the ecosystem building on, and attributing, the official text.

What I Am Asking

I propose six measures, each designed to ensure the fifth edition is supported by working citation tools from the date of publication:

  1. Share draft rules with tool implementers under NDA during drafting. Low-cost for the Committee, and it allows implementation issues to be identified while minor clarifications are still possible.
  2. Accept implementation-feedback memoranda at drafting milestones. Translating rules into deterministic software logic is a rigorous test of their clarity; structured feedback identifying rules that resist automation, with alternative formulations that preserve intent, would strengthen the finished guide. The catalogue summarised above is the first such memorandum.
  3. Publish an official machine-readable companion to the printed guide. A structured expression of the rules, or even the sample citation library as an official Committee artefact, would collapse the time between publication and working tool support, and under the licensing model above it is also a revenue line rather than a cost.
  4. Ship an authoritative erratum process with the edition, and release the Summary of Changes before publication rather than after, so tool authors can prepare implementations before the guide reaches users, and so the example bands, the fourth edition’s primary defect surface, are reconciled against their rules from day one.
  5. Invite the open-source citation community to an implementer review. A single session, even informal, would yield substantial returns in implementation quality.
  6. Adopt an open licence for the text of the guide, paired with commercial licensing of the machine-readable ruleset, conformance suite, and certification, per the model above.

What I Am Offering

In return, I can offer the Committee a concrete testing service: Obiter’s engine covers all 26 chapters, backed by around 3,300 tests pinned to the guide’s own rules and examples. Any draft AGLC5 rule shared under NDA can be encoded, tested against real citation data, and returned with a structured report identifying ambiguities, edge cases, and formatting conflicts before the rule reaches publication. The supporting research is already public in the Obiter repository: the consolidated defect catalogue, a register of every point at which the tool was forced beyond the guide, a modern-sources proposal with worked element structures for AI output, datasets, and software, a primary-source review of Australian court citation practice directions, and a comparative study of how OSCOLA, the Bluebook, the McGill Guide, APA, MLA, Chicago, and the ICMJE modernised.

Beyond testing, I offer structured feedback on draft rules as they develop, and a public commitment to making AGLC5 support free and available to every Australian researcher, student, and practitioner from the day the edition is published. I am not asking for a seat on the Committee. I am asking for the input data that tool authors need to do their best work, and offering my work in return.

The AGLC has been indispensable to Australian legal citation for over 25 years, and the open-source community’s investment in AGLC4 tooling is itself evidence of the standard’s importance. AGLC5 can be the strongest edition yet; a small amount of collaboration during drafting, and one forward-looking licensing decision, will make a meaningful difference to how quickly and accurately the new rules reach the researchers who depend on them. I am available to discuss any of this at the Committee’s convenience, by email, video call, or in person in Melbourne.

Respectfully,
